**Research Information Sucks! Why Nobody Loves It — and Why Openness Is the Only Way Out!**  
Philipp Steglich	Leibniz Association)
>Research information is unpopular across the board — but not for the same reasons. Researchers see it as bureaucratic overhead. Organisations struggle with fragmented systems and unclear responsibilities. Policy actors view it as a governance tool, yet do little to enforce binding standards. The real common denominator: every stakeholder group sees only its own slice of research information — its own systems, its own use cases. A connecting overall picture, and a shared understanding of the value of high-quality open research information, exist almost nowhere. If nobody fully understands the object and nobody recognises a benefit in it for themselves, nobody takes responsibility for it either. 
While case studies documenting institutional implementation give important insights, this talk asks: why are these efforts so fragile and unevenly distributed? Half-hearted openness has not solved this. Voluntary commitments and partial standards have normalised fragmentation and lock-in to proprietary systems. Only radical openness, full metadata transparency, interoperability, open infrastructures and collective governance can break the cycle. In the age of AI, where machine-readable information determines visibility and reputation, this is no longer normative preference but strategic necessity. Open infrastructures are preconditions for open, democratic societies.
Drawing on experience from the German research system, particularly non-university research, the talk closes with a concrete claim: the real unresolved bottleneck is not technology but persuasion. Research information must be treated as a governance question, not an IT problem — requiring all stakeholders, from researchers to institutions, funders and policy-makers, to genuinely adopt open, interoperable research information rather than merely regard it as an unavoidable burden. A declaration only turns into actual practice once each of these groups understands what they concretely gain from doing so.
>
>This talk reframes policy-practice alignment as a persuasion problem, not a technical one. Declarations like Barcelona set the norm, but actors with functioning isolated data holdings have no inherent reason to open up. Practice only follows once every stakeholder group — researchers, institutions, funders, policy-makers — sees a concrete benefit: visibility, reduced duplicate reporting, comparability, evidence-based steering.

**Pathways to Implementation: Practical Lessons from the University of Amsterdam**  
Manuel Llano*	(University of Amsterdam)
>This presentation reports the results of a three-month project investigating the pathways for implementation of the Barcelona Declaration's commitments at the University of Amsterdam. The project examined the organisational, technical, and policy avenues through which the Declaration’s principles will be translated into practice. The study consisted of three components. Firstly, we developed an inventory of relevant research information systems, data sources, workflows, and policies across the university. This mapping identified where existing practices and systems already align with the Declaration and where further changes and additions are needed. Secondly, we assessed the quality and coverage of research information from open and closed sources for our use cases. The comparison focused on completeness, reliability, consistency, and usability, with particular attention to whether open sources can support institutional needs currently met through proprietary systems. Thirdly, an end-user qualitative survey (n=120) explored how researchers and other relevant stakeholders, use research information and how they perceive the accessibility, quality, and usefulness of different sources. This is broken down by role and disciplinary background. 
>
>This proposal directly addresses the conference theme by examining how the Barcelona Declaration’s policy commitments can be translated into institutional practice. Drawing on an inventory of systems and policies, a comparison of open and closed data sources, and end-user empirical research, it showcases the approach chosen by the University of Amsterdam and draws lessons that other institutions and prospective signatories can use as a stepping stone for developing implementation pathways adapted to their own organisational contexts.

**From commitment to implementation: Context, considerations and next steps for the University of Zurich**  
Melanie Röthlisberger* / Anita Sbalzerini* (University of Zurich)	
>The University of Zurich (UZH) signed the Barcelona Declaration in summer 2025 following discussion within its Working Group (WG) Open Science, a cross-faculty body bringing together representatives from all faculties, early-career researchers, library directors, and members of the Executive Board. The discussion at UZH was informed by the university’s disciplinary breadth and by concerns about the limitations of proprietary research information sources. In particular, the WG highlighted the disciplinary and linguistic biases of commercial databases such as Web of Science, as well as the implications of increasingly closed research information infrastructures. The Barcelona Declaration was considered in the context of UZH’s existing commitments to DORA and CoARA and as an opportunity to advance discussion about research information and the use of open alternatives within the university. Signing the Declaration was therefore not an endpoint but is the starting point for further institutional work and discussions. UZH has identified several next steps, some of which we have already started to implement. These include: assessing the use of Web of Science and Scopus across faculties and central services, including consideration of the costs and benefits of closed infrastructures; expanding the institutional repository ZORA to include research outputs beyond publications; and participating in the NAIF project – a national project that aims to increase interoperability across repositories. Planned next steps for the rest of 2026 and 2027 include assessing the quality of open databases for UZH research outputs and developing an action plan for implementation.
>
>The presentation discusses how an institutional governance structure can connect a policy commitment to practical implementation, while also illustrating the questions and challenges that emerge when moving from a declaration of principle towards changes in research information practices and infrastructures.

**Exploring Institutional Pathways for Implementing the Barcelona Declaration**   
Debora do Nascimento* / Fabiana de Melo Amaral / Ivanilma Gama / Daniel de Oliveira / Josimara Dias Brumatti (Universidade Federal Fluminense, Brazil)	
>The Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information proposes that information used in the management, monitoring, and evaluation of research should be accessible, transparent, and reusable. However, formal endorsement does not, by itself, ensure its incorporation into institutional policies, infrastructures, and processes.  This contribution presents a framework to support universities and research institutions in the progressive implementation of the Declaration’s commitments, taking the experience of Fluminense Federal University, the first Brazilian university to become a signatory, as its reference. The study adopts a qualitative, descriptive, and propositional approach, based on documentary research on the institutional trajectory of Open Science at UFF, including regulations, action plans, information infrastructures, and initiatives related to training, communication, and intersectoral coordination. The proposal derives from the Open Science Institutionalization Framework, developed through the experience of the UFF Open Science Center. The model organizes institutionalization around actors, structural dimensions, implementation stages, and maturity levels, while integrating governance, infrastructure, services, and culture. It also includes institutional diagnosis, stakeholder mobilization, organizational structuring, regulatory formalization, monitoring, and systemic integration. For its application to the Barcelona Declaration, these components are connected to the commitments to make openness the default, work with open systems and services, support sustainable infrastructures, and promote collective action. The contribution argues that implementing open research information requires governance, systems integration, defined responsibilities, capacity building, sustainability, and transparent monitoring. It proposes an adaptable framework to guide institutions from formal endorsement to effective implementation.	
>
>The proposal is directly related to the topic by demonstrating how the commitments of the Barcelona Declaration can be translated into institutional practices. Drawing on the experience of UFF, the framework integrates policies, governance, infrastructure, services, and cultural change into progressive stages of implementation. In this way, it bridges the gap between international guidelines and the concrete realities of universities, providing a tool to identify gaps, define responsibilities, plan actions, and monitor the incorporation of open research information.

**Stanford Center for Open Metadata, Open Metadata Policy Toolkit, and Reality on the Ground: a US Perspective**  
Tom Cramer* / Rochelle Lundy* / Alexis Manheim (Stanford University Libraries, United States)

>The Stanford Center for Open Metadata, based in Stanford’s University Libraries, is committed to making open metadata for all types of assets – articles, books, cultural heritage, datasets, educational resources, and more – the global norm for research, education and cultural heritage. One of the Center’s key initiatives is advocating for open metadata policies at institutional and national levels. Grounded in our own experience of writing a Stanford policy in 2025 as well as conversations with dozens of other institutions across the world, we have recently released a directory of open metadata policies and a toolkit to assist libraries and other institutions in writing their own. This session will describe the experience of working to open metadata from our US research library perspective, including 1) details on the Open Metadata Policy Toolkit, 2) the principles and messaging that resonate with colleagues across a broad range of functional areas within a library, and 3) the opportunities and challenges of applying open principles in contract negotiations with our suppliers. Although we encountered some practical and philosophical turbulence in moving toward open metadata, we discovered that adopting a policy galvanized interest in pockets of our institution where we did not expect to see relevance and led to progress in areas where we did not anticipate easy change. As our focus includes all metadata, not just research information, it affords an opportunity for the Barcelona Declaration community to compare and contrast context and approaches with an analogous section of the global metadata ecosystem.
>
>This session details the impact of adopting an institutional open metadata policy on services and procedures in several parts of our organization. It also covers how developing and implementing a policy can be a catalyst to changing behaviors and norms across the library sector.

**From publishing policy to open research information: redesigning workflows, governance, and decision-making at University of Galway**  
Cillian Joy* / Monica Crump (University of Galway, Ireland)
>As a signatory to the Barcelona Declaration, University of Galway provides a useful case of how open research information policy intersects with research publishing practice. The University adopted a new Research Publishing Policy in December 2025 to connect research quality, immediate open access, rights retention, repository deposit, and institutional publishing strategy. It mandates rights retention and requires staff and students to make publications openly available on acceptance, normally through deposit of the accepted manuscript or Version of Record. Implementation has shown that policy is only effective when translated into research-information workflows. The University is redesigning the path from CRIS to repository so that author, affiliation, funder, rights, licence, version and persistent-identifier metadata can support deposit, discovery, monitoring, and reporting. Responsibility is distributed across the Library, Research and Innovation Office and Institutional Research and Planning Office, with governance through the Research and Innovation Committee. A second challenge concerns the evidence used to guide publishing decisions. The policy asks researchers to target domain-leading outlets with a view to raising the profile and reputation of the University.  However, operationalising this can reproduce dependence on proprietary rankings and commercial metrics. The University is therefore developing a community-governed model in which disciplinary judgement, transparent criteria and research information sources are combined, aiming to reduce reliance on closed indicators and make decisions more auditable.  This presentation reflects on these implementation choices, tensions, and unfinished work. It argues that open research information becomes meaningful institutionally when embedded in publishing services, repository and CRIS workflows, and governance processes rather than treated as a separate information-policy agenda.
>
>The proposal examines how University of Galway is translating an institutional Research Publishing Policy into operational practice. It focuses on the governance, CRIS-to-repository workflows, metadata, rights and decision processes needed to make policy actionable. It also considers how publishing decisions can move towards more transparent and open research information. The case shows where policy creates new requirements for systems and services, and where implementation experience exposes gaps and informs further policy and governance development.

**Metadata, Negotiation Mandates and Dashboards: Barcelona Declaration in Practice at Couperin consortium**  
Adeline Rege* / Marlène Delhaye (Université de Strasbourg / Couperin consortium, France)
>Couperin, the French national consortium for higher education and research libraries, signed the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information in March 2026. This presentation looks at how that commitment translates into two concrete strands of the consortium's everyday work: negociations with publishers and data monitoring and reporting.
On the negotiation side, the negotiation mandates prepared ahead of each negotiation with major scholarly publishers now systematically embed requirements on the provision of open metadata to the national tools provided by Abes, the French bibliographic agency. This means that the bibliographic and licensing metadata generated by consortium agreements are made available in open, reusable form, rather than remaining locked inside publisher or vendor platforms. We will use recent agreements to show how this requirement is negotiated in practice, and its results and limits. On the monitoring and reporting side, Couperin relies on open data from OpenAlex, mobilised through the open source visualization tool LODEX, to build indicators without depending on proprietary bibliometric sources. The results of the two annual national surveys led by Couperin (on national e-resources expenditure and on institutional APC expenditure) are published as open data and have a dedicated LODEX dashboard. Finally, Couperin also reports its members collective funding of open science infrastructures into the TSOSI (Transparency to Sustain Open Science Infrastructure) platform, contributing to the transparency of the funding landscape rather than only consuming open data. Taken together, these 3 examples illustrate what it concretely takes for a library consortium to move from signing the Declaration to embedding it into recurring workflows: which clauses to negotiate, which tools and internal processes to set up, and which tensions arise between ambition and practical feasibility.
>
>The proposal illustrates what it concretely takes for a library consortium to move from signing the Declaration to embedding it into recurring workflows: which clauses to negotiate, which tools and internal processes to set up, and which tensions arise between ambition and practical feasibility. The presentation aims to be a practice-sharing contribution, directly addressing the conference's focus on the interplay between policy and practice.

**Transitioning from proprietary to open bibliographic databases - Experiences for the Netherlands**  
Ludo Waltman (Centre for Science and Technology Studies (CWTS), Leiden University) / Jeroen Bosman* (Utrecht University) / Joeri Both (VU Amsterdam) / Hans de Jonge (Dutch Research Council NWO) / Matthijs de Zwaan (VU Amsterdam) / Mariëlle Prevoo (Maastricht University) / Ana Ranitovic* (University of Groningen) / Franklin van der Hoeven (Delft University of Technology)
>In the Netherlands a number of research organizations supporting the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information recently made the decision to discontinue their subscription to the Web of Science and/or Scopus bibliographic databases. This includes five universities (Delft University of Technology, Maastricht University, University of Groningen, Utrecht University, VU Amsterdam), the Dutch Research Council (NWO), and the Centre for Science and Technology Studies (CWTS). These organizations have started to rely more strongly on open bibliographic databases, in particular OpenAlex and/or OpenAIRE. At the same time, most of these organizations still have a subscription to a proprietary database, either Web of Science or Scopus, although various organizations are evaluating whether they still need this subscription. The proposed talk will provide an overview of the recent developments in the Netherlands in transitioning from proprietary to open bibliographic databases. We will share experiences from the organizations involved, discussing how the transition was organized internally and how decision making took place. We will also reflect on the consequences of discontinuing subscriptions to proprietary bibliographic databases, in particular in terms of responses from researchers and other staff, experiences with open databases, and cost savings. By sharing the lessons learned by Dutch research organizations, this talk aims to support signatories of the Barcelona Declaration in the implementation of their transition from proprietary to open bibliographic databases.
>
>The proposed contribution shows how research organizations in the Netherlands have moved from the policy decision to sign the Barcelona Declaration to the practical action of discontinuing their subscription to a proprietary bibliographic database.

**Who Orients Science? Territorial Layers and Specialised Functions in the Open Research Information Ecosystem**  
Gabriel Vélez-Cuartas (Universidad de Antioquia, Colombia)
>Replacing proprietary sources is not a matter of swapping one database for another: it depends on whether an ecosystem dense enough to sustain the substitution can be traced. Drawing on the Colombian CRIS ImpactU and on comparative work on publishing strategies in the region, this presentation shows that such an ecosystem is already observable. It is ordered by territorial levels — institutional repositories; national systems such as ImpactU, BrCris and PerúCRIS; regional networks including LA Referencia, Redalyc, SciELO, Latindex and OpenAIRE; and global aggregators such as OpenAlex, Crossref, DOAJ and OpenCitations — and by specialised functions: identification, curation, aggregation, standardisation and stewardship, underpinned by open code, open data, shared funding and data sovereignty. No layer is viable alone; interdependencies run upwards as metadata and downwards as contextualisation, and this is the practical condition of replacement. The second claim concerns orientation. Commercial systems do not merely supply data, they steer research. Relevant knowledge becomes visible only through indexation decisions taken by firms that, using data appropriated from academic communities, build automated, AI-driven tools capturing researchers’ attention through criteria of validity grounded in metrics rather than community-building. Colombian evidence illustrates the consequence: publishing in a high-profile hybrid journal triggers identical institutional rewards whether an article gathers thirteen thousand citations or none, while channelling public funds to commercial actors. Left unchecked, commercial firms will define what counts as scientific, wresting that authority from academic communities. Open infrastructures should therefore not reproduce commercial metrics free of charge, but answer different questions — about real contribution, social usefulness and socio-territorial capacities — returning the definition of scientific value to research communities.
>
>This presentation draws on a regional case: ImpactU, a Colombian CRIS operating within a layered open ecosystem that enables substitution. It reports both success and setback — infrastructure that works technically, alongside national incentive policies that still reward proprietary indexation. It addresses the proposed topic directly, while touching on governance, AI dependencies and research assessment. It contributes a Global South perspective, arguing that replacement depends on interdependent territorial layers and specialised functions rather than on a single open database.

**Creating an institutional ecosystem for Open Research Information: OpenCitations and the University of Bologna**  
Chiara Di Giambattista (OpenCitations / University of Bologna, Italy) 
>The transition towards Open Research Information requires institutions not only to adopt open infrastructures but also to develop coherent governance models and institutional policies supporting their long-term sustainability. The University of Bologna (UNIBO) is a relevant case study, as its commitment to Open Science has progressively evolved into a strategy for adopting and promoting Open Research Information through institutional initiatives, including the endorsement of the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information. UNIBO supports this transition by sustaining open scholarly infrastructures and using open services and systems for managing, disseminating and monitoring research information. UNIBO hosts the Research Centre for Open Scholarly Metadata, handling the administrative and management activities of OpenCitations, an open infrastructure providing open citation data and a supporter of the Barcelona Declaration. Recently, OpenCitations underwent a governance redesign, developed by a working group of its International Advisory Board with the UNIBO legal offices. This resulted in the formal recognition of the University as the Hosting Member of OpenCitations, reinforcing its institutional commitment while establishing the legal foundations for long-term sustainability and ensuring that its community-led values can endure beyond its original leadership. UNIBO has also experimented with Open Research Information services (OpenAIRE Monitor) to support monitoring of research outputs and projects through open metadata. In the past two years, internal activities have explored Open Research Information systems as alternatives to proprietary services, combining this information with its Current Research Information System IRIS. Preliminary outcomes show that data from Open Research Information sources can satisfactorily address tasks previously carried out using proprietary sources such as Web of Science and Scopus.
>
>The University of Bologna case demonstrates how the endorsement of principles such as those expressed in the Barcelona Declaration is supported by the institutional adoption of open infrastructures and systems. By focusing on the governance evolution of OpenCitations, the proposal highlights how policy commitments can extend beyond strategic statements to shape governance models and the long-term sustainability of open research infrastructures.

**Open Infrastructure Funding as Open Research Information: TSOSI's First Year in Practice**  
Maxence Larrieu (TSOSI /GRICAD, Université Grenoble Alpes, France)
>TSOSI (Transparency to Sustain Open Science Infrastructure) is a recent initiative whose web platform (tsosi.org) was launched in June 2025. Its goal is to make financial support for open infrastructures commonplace. TSOSI’s original approach is to treat financial support for open infrastructures as a form of research information, and to make it openly available. TSOSI allows anyone to answer questions such as: who supported DOAJ in 2025? What is the regional diversity of PKP's financial supporters? When did this university start supporting DOAB? After an introduction, we will present TSOSI's “funding network of open science infrastructures”, connecting 43 infrastructures, 21 research funders, 35 library consortia, and more than 1,000 performing research organisations (as of August 2026). One originality of TSOSI is that it reveals the links between this diversity of organisations and infrastructures, showing how research communities have organized themselves to sustain open infrastructures. In the second part, we will share what we have learned from the organisations we work with. TSOSI produces a new type of open research information, describing financial support for open infrastructures.  We have mainly collected data from three types of organisations: open infrastructures, library consortia, and research institutions. We will show how these organisations manage this information along four dimensions: volume, velocity, richness, and software used. This framework can then be used to facilitate discussions with participants and inform the TSOSI strategy. We will close by outlining key milestones for 2026–2027, and by inviting discussion on how TSOSI can be used at the institutional level to both practise information sharing and help shape policy in favour of open infrastructures.
>
>TSOSI's originality lies in treating financial support for open infrastructures as a form of open research information. It invites institutions to put information sharing into practice, and our proposal will also focus on how tsosi.org is actually used within research institutions. Ultimately, TSOSI aims to inform decision-making around open infrastructure funding policy; by making information widely available, it helps shape policy in favour of sustainable open infrastructures.

**Open Infrastructures Under Pressure: AI, Impact and Sustainability**  
Dominic Mitchell (DOAJ)
>DOAJ is a signatory of the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information and adheres to the Principles of Open Scholarly Infrastructures, both initiatives that promote openness, transparency and accountability. They are clear signals to the community that DOAJ can be trusted. However, in today’s open scholarly ecosystem, “open” comes with real challenges for open infrastructures. The challenges of high-volume web scraping to feed AI models have become well known to libraries and Open Access infrastructures. DOAJ users deal with slowdowns and downtime, while our server and staff costs increase to handle this new normal of bot traffic. While AI-bot attacks seem to be endless, DOAJ's funding is not. AI companies take advantage of freely available collections and high-quality metadata without giving back to the ecosystem. As academic and funding organisations face more initiatives to fund, requirements to secure funding increasingly rely on impact. For open infrastructures, measuring and proving impact can be a huge challenge. DOAJ is completely open and free to use, including our metadata endpoints. How can we prove our impact without tracking our community? AI, measuring and proving impact, an increasing number of applications, and community demands for better-quality metadata mean rising costs. Open infrastructures are challenged to cover these costs equitably. In this presentation, we will explore these challenges and suggest strategies to tackle them while staying true to the promises we make to support open metadata and open infrastructures.
>
>DOAJ is open by default and always has been. Our policy and mission are to share scholarly research information openly. In practice, we find that characteristics of today's scholarly research ecosystem force us to make changes that challenge that policy. It's no longer a simple case of open or closed.

**From fragmented funding data to a shared Open Funding Metadata Framework: lessons from an Italian interregional community of practice.**  
Elisabetta Maini* (Regione Emilia-Romagna, Italy)	
>Regional governments play an important role in implementing research and innovation policies, yet information on what they fund, why they fund it, and what these investments produce remains fragmented across systems and jurisdictions. This limits the capacity to use research information for strategic monitoring, policy learning and cooperation.
This presentation shares the experience of a community of practice of Italian regional governments translating open research information principles into a shared framework for funding metadata and R&I monitoring. Created around the implementation and monitoring of Smart Specialisation Strategies (S3), the initiative grew through four workshops in 2025–2026, from 11 founding regions to the active engagement of 18 of Italy’s 20 regional administrations. Rather than starting from technical standards, the process began with shared policy needs. Through peer learning, regions explored how open metadata, persistent identifiers and interoperability could connect funding with organisations, projects and research outputs. The resulting framework promotes interoperable funding metadata, persistent identifiers and stronger interoperability across regional, national and European systems.  A distinctive feature is its emphasis on the policy context of funding instruments. For regional governments, whose R&I investments are closely linked to territorial development and expected economic, social and environmental impacts, knowing what has been funded is not enough: projects and outputs need to be connected to the policy objectives, priorities and instruments that generated them. The experience highlights a key lesson for implementing the Barcelona Declaration: moving from principles to practice requires not only open infrastructures and standards, but also awareness, institutional capacity and trust. Communities of practice can help turn open research information into better, more coordinated and evidence-informed R&I policy.	
>
>Our proposal directly addresses the conference theme by demonstrating how policy ambitions for open research information can be translated into practical implementation. Through an interregional community of practice supporting S3 Strategies, Italian regional governments moved from shared policy needs to the co-design of a common framework for funding metadata, identifiers and interoperability. The experience highlights how collaboration, capacity building and governance mechanisms can bridge the gap between policy objectives and operational adoption of open research information.

**Providing Open, AI-Ready Information on Institutional Funding Activities and Integrating It with Existing Funding Data**  
Philipp Steglich / Matthias Goeritz / Anja Görnitz* / Christian Hauschke (Leibniz Association, Germany)
>The Leibniz Association connects 96 independent German research institutions ranging in focus from natural, engineering and environmental sciences to economics, spatial and social sciences and the humanities. Information on third-party funded projects at Leibniz institutes (as well as German research institutions in general) is currently scattered across multiple platforms. This project explores potentials to harvest funding metadata from diverse funders to consolidate these into a filterable, exportable open research portal (“LORIS Projects”) and integrate own metadata from the Leibniz Association’s strategic funding programmes.
Funding metadata provides structured, comparable, up-to-date information on research topics and collaborations. It is a useful data source to map (part of) research institutions’ current research activities. As some funders (e. g. EU funding bodies) already provide reusable and machine-readable metadata, it could be possible to find a cost-effective way for umbrella organizations to harvest these existing metadata across funders. This would minimize duplicate research efforts, facilitate reporting activities and strengthen external visibility. The project team currently focuses on providing open metadata on own funding activities (35 million EUR per year) and harvesting metadata provided by EU funding bodies. Possibilities to harvest funding metadata of all relevant national and international funders, especially DFG, shall be explored in the future. The open funding metadata shall be provided via the open source software VIVO, which is continuously being improved by TIB (Leibniz Information Centre for Science and Technology – University Library). Insights gained and new features developed as part of LORIS Projects will also contribute to VIVO's further development and be made available to the community.
>
>This talk addresses challenges of building processes and an infrastructure that meet the commitments set out in the Barcelona Declaration.

**Opening grant metadata: the role of philanthropic funders in building open research information**  
Paola Isetta* / Montserrat Capdevila (“la Caixa” Foundation, Spain)	
>Philanthropic funders are important producers of research information. Through their funding activities, they generate metadata about grants, researchers, organisations and outputs, as well as information that helps trace research trajectories over time. Making this information available and interoperable can improve understanding of research systems, funding flows and the role of philanthropy within them. As a signatory of the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information, the “la Caixa” Foundation (LCF) is exploring how to translate these principles into its Open Science strategy and funding practices. LCF’s research funding encompasses different instruments: research and innovation projects, fellowships, and support to research-performing organisations. Linking them to people, organisations and outputs requires a coherent metadata. An institutional diagnosis and participatory process identified grant metadata as a strategic area for development. LCF is therefore exploring a common metadata framework and ways to connect this information with international research information infrastructures. This process raises broader questions for philanthropic funders: which metadata should be collected and shared across funding instruments; how to establish governance mechanism to ensure data quality and long-term maintenance; how grants, people, organisations and outputs can be reliably connected; how these relationships can remain traceable beyond funding periods; how open grant metadata can support transparency, portfolio analysis, institutional learning and evidence-informed decision-making. The contribution will present LCF’s emerging approach, its challenges and the role of philanthropic funders as producers and stewards of open research funding information. By making grant metadata available and interoperable, they can strengthen the open research information ecosystem and enhance transparency, accountability and understanding of the research they support.	
>

**The Mandate Says Deposit, the Institution Hears Risk: SURDATA and the Language of Open Research Information**  
Rodrigo Roa López de Heredia (Data Observatory Foundation, Chile)
>Chile has a mandate. Since 2022 the national research agency has required grantees, across 29 funding instruments, to deposit publications and data, submit a data management plan and report the DOI of each article, with non-compliance tied to future transfers of funds.
It is a mandate to deposit, not to describe. It says nothing about how institutions should identify or connect what they deposit, and asks for a DOI for the article, never the dataset. For public sector data, where much of our research input lives, there is no technical rulebook at all: no schema, no identifiers, no rules on how a state publishes. And when a public institution here is told to open its data, the first thing it hears is exposure and complaint.
SURDATA, the open and responsible data alliance from the Global South, with Data Observatory as its technical secretariat, works in that gap, and its most useful move was a change of language. We stopped asking institutions to open their data and began asking that it be findable, documented, traceable and citable, with the access decision left to them. Separating the access question, which is political, from the description question, which is not, is what makes FAIR workable for a state that wants its institutions to interoperate. Practice follows, bottom up, one institution against a state's data. We built GEMA, a metadata enrichment tool, and run an open catalogue of more than a thousand datasets. We assign DOIs to digital artefacts, databases, platforms, reports and now legislative records: Chile has two DataCite consortia, dividing by object rather than competing. And SURDATA trains the people who will have to do this inside their own institutions. We will report what has not worked: metadata arrives inconsistent, custodianship diffuse because signing means answering. Then a question to the room. A mandate without infrastructure produces compliance without interoperability. Public data has neither. Who supplies what a mandate assumes?
>
>Chile shows both halves out of step. ANID's 2022 open access policy binds 29 funding instruments and ties non-compliance to future transfers, yet asks for a DOI for the article and not the dataset. For public sector data there is no technical rule on how a state publishes. SURDATA supplies the missing practice from below: a change of language separating the access decision from the duty to describe, metadata tooling, an open catalogue, DOIs for digital artefacts, and training. Practice returned to policy in submissions arguing that law should require data to be identifiable and citable.

**From open research information to an open research information commons**  
 Eileen Waegemaekers* (SURF,  The Netherlands)
> The call for open research information (ORI) is growing louder, yet the pathway to a sustainable, community‑driven open research information space is not straightforward. There is fragmentation, diverse stakeholder needs, and dominance of commercial vendors (e.g., Elsevier, Clarivate). Building on Elinor Ostrom’s Governing the Commons, we propose a commons approach that places ownership, decision‑making, and long‑term stewardship in the hands of the users who create and consume research information.
A commons approach has three ingredients: 1. Community: in the case of ORI these are creators and consumers of research information, namely researchers, librarians, research support staff, funders, and policymakers who co-define the purpose and scope of the ORI commons;  2. The shared resources and facilities: the actual research information (resources) and the tools and services to create, manage and maintain the resources (facilities); 3. The act of commoning: the collective action that seeks to generate and manage the shared resources and facilities. In ORI these can be processes for adopting or adapting standards, collectively deciding on which licenses to cancel and how to build alternatives, or creating shared funding pools for sustaining infrastructure.  The SURF ORI program is currently experimenting with the commons by applying it in three projects: MORIS (project-centric RIS), Broccoli (datahub for analytics), and DURF (federation of repositories). With input from these projects we develop a profile using the Global Open Research Commons model and identify concrete points for collective action.
In this lightning talk we illustrate how the commons approach encourages reinforcement between policy and practice, by embedding decision‑making close to the communities we ensure that the ecosystem becomes responsive to evolving workflows and ultimately that knowledge can flow without constraints.
>
>Policy and practice within a commons framework are realised through collective action. By working with the community we co‑define how we organise all elements needed for an ORI commons (GORC model). The SURF ORI projects (MORIS, Broccoli, and DURF) act as live testbeds where policy specifications (e.g., open licences, standard adoption, completeness of metadata) are put into practice, monitored, and continuously improved by researchers, librarians, funders, and policymakers together. This iterative feedback loop shows how policy shapes practice and, in turn, how practice informs and refines.

**From Cedram to Centre Mersenne: A 15-Year Journey of Growth with open metadata**  
Murielle Serlet* (centre Mersenne - CNRS, France)
>The entre Mersenne is a Diamond Open Access publishing platform offering all tools for editorial teams to manage, produce and disseminate publications. All our metadata are open and interoperable: OAI-PMH protocols, metadata export, DOI attribution via Crossref, etc. It exemplifies how practical adoption of open metadata standards aligns with open policy goals. Since our first DOI assignment in 2011 — when we were known as Cedram — our collaboration with Crossref has evolved from basic identifier registration to a comprehensive approach. In 2018, when Cedram became the centre Mersenne, our practices evolved: we began registering metadata (titles, authors, bibliographies), later expanding to include publishers, abstracts, ORCID IDs, and licenses by 2025. Recognizing the growing importance of open metadata in databases like OpenAlex, we progressively enriched our metadata to include publishers, abstracts, Creative Commons licenses and ORCID IDs. This evolution has transformed how our content is discovered, trusted, and reused.
In 2018, we integrated Crossref’s API to match references, improving data quality and reproducibility. Since 2021, the Cited-by feature has enabled hosted journals to track scholarly impact. The centre Mersenne also uses ORCID identifiers to enrich all its references. The use of open metadata is crucial for indexing publications. We use the OAI-PMH protocol to transmit our data to scientific databases. In addition, we also develop tools to improve indexing. For example, we developed a custom tool based on open metadata to identify articles missing from NASA ADS, ensuring that our journals are included in key databases.These tools not only enhance our services but also strengthen the scholarly open ecosystem by making connections between publications more transparent. By adopting and enriching open metadata, we have increased our visibility and, thanks to the quality of our indexing, contributed to the reliability of these databases.
>
>Our evolution exemplifies how practical adoption of open metadata standards aligns with policy goals of the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information. Indeed, we are implementing open standards (DOI, ORCID, OAI-PMH), that are key requirements of Open Science policies. We are also applying metadata enrichment for open, interoperable and reusable research information. Our tools (Cited-by, OJS, custom tools) operationalize the open policies. This demonstrates how technical implementation supports policy objectives, replacing proprietary dependencies with open, reusable infrastructure.

**Bridging Open Data: An Open Research Data Aggregator for Institutions**  
Emily Bongiovanni* / Kristen Scotti / Jonathan Kiritharan / Erin Higgins (Carnegie Mellon University, United States)
>Carnegie Mellon University Libraries has developed a prototype metadata aggregator for open research datasets. This project is inspired by the OA Switchboard model, which serves as a hub for standardized Open Access publication metadata records, shared at the institutional member level. This open data connector serves as a central hub for collecting research data metadata records across repositories and segments it at the institutional level. By delivering the metadata at the institution level, this prototype enables organizations to track and link to externally hosted research data. Carnegie Mellon University Libraries uses this metadata to generate local institutional repository records that point directly to external host repositories. This eliminates collecting and storage demands for the Libraries, while still capturing a more comprehensive and holistic picture of the university's scientific footprint. Scaled globally, this prototype will offer a streamlined, automated method for institutions and funders to assess compliance with data-sharing mandates. This poster demonstrates the functional prototype design, outlines its technical framework, and invites community feedback and collaboration from stakeholders seeking to support or use the platform.
>
>This project commits to open research information via a practical open-source tool. It ensures metadata is decentralized, interoperable, and immediately usable by participating institutions. Funders and institutions increasingly mandate open data sharing, but tracking compliance across hundreds of external repositories is manually unsustainable. This prototype automates that pipeline, converting high-level policy expectations into actionable, trackable metadata feeds.
