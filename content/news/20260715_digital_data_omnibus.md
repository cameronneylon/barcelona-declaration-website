The Digital Omnibus on Data, the first major piece of legislation to reach the European Parliament and Council in the 2024-2029 mandate, is being built largely around the needs of business.

That leaves two gaps for research and libraries: a definition of ‘research data’ that does not yet cover the metadata needed to find and assess research, and equal protection against unfair contract terms for research institutions seeking to access data. Knowledge Rights 21 (KR21) is calling on the European Parliament and Council to close both gaps as the file moves forward.

The Omnibus itself is branded as a simplification exercise, merging the Data Governance Act, the Open Data Directive, and the Data Act, and amending the General Data Protection Regulation, to give data-intensive sectors a single rule-book to follow.

### Enabling Better Research Assessment and Discoverability

One of the pre-existing pieces of legislation to be absorbed is the Open Data Directive, agreed in 2019 to update the EU’s rules on public sector information.

A key innovation with the new Directive was to provide that all research outputs (except final publications) be made open access by default, under an open licence, according to the principle of ‘as open as possible, as closed as necessary’. In the context of the Directive, these outputs are collectively known as ‘research data’. 

This marked a powerful step towards open science, and indeed represented the [first binding legal intervention](https://knowledgerights21.org/wp-content/uploads/KR21-Insights-Opening-Up-Open-Access.pdf) made by the EU in this area, outside of regulations concerning the EU’s own research spending.

The Digital Omnibus offers the opportunity to build on this work by clarifying that ‘research data’ also includes research information: the metadata about research that facilitates discoverability, research assessment, and both analysis and decision-making about research. This includes titles, abstracts, references, author data, affiliation data, funding data, and information on organisations and contributors. 

KR21 has worked closely with the [Barcelona Declaration](https://barcelona-declaration.org) to propose changes that would achieve this goal by clarifying that the Directive’s definition of research data (which already goes far beyond ‘pure’ data) also includes research information.

Through this, the EU can support wider efforts to reform research assessment, facilitate the discoverability of research, and make it easier for all actors, not just big publishers, to understand broader trends in the research space.

### A Fairer Deal for Libraries and Their Users

KR21 has [already highlighted](https://www.techpolicy.press/what-the-eus-digital-omnibus-means-for-researchers/)  concerns that the Digital Omnibus does not do enough to support the interests of researchers and other library users when it comes to access to data. 

The Commission’s draft fails to address the anomaly in EU law whereby businesses are offered extensive protection against unfair contract terms when seeking to obtain data, while research institutions can only do so indirectly, in emergencies. The proposed text even makes this access more difficult than it was the case before.

KR21 is therefore calling for steps to ensure consistency between the EU’s strong policy and financial commitment to research, and its legislation around data. Research institutions should benefit from at least the same level of protection against unfair contract terms as businesses, and should have much wider possibilities to access data in the first place.

In addition, we support [Communia’s call](https://communia-association.org/2026/07/06/open-letter-protect-open-standard-licences-for-public-sector-information/) to safeguard standardised open licensing, which further proposals on differential licensing depending on the users of open government data would undermine.

We look forward to engaging with Members of the European Parliament and other decision makers to ensure that the Digital Omnibus represents a further step forward for open science and access in Europe, rather than a missed opportunity.
